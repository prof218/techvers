Here's an uncomfortable truth about breaking into AI: nobody is impressed that you *finished a course*. What hiring managers want is evidence you can take a problem and ship something that works. That evidence is your portfolio — and most candidates build theirs wrong.

This guide shows you how to build one that actually gets interviews.

## Why most portfolios fail

The typical beginner portfolio is a pile of tutorial notebooks: the Titanic dataset, MNIST digits, a copied RAG demo. The problem isn't that these are bad exercises — it's that *everyone* has them. They prove you can follow instructions, not that you can think.

A portfolio that gets hired does three things a tutorial never will:

1. Solves a believable, slightly original problem
2. Shows your reasoning and trade-offs
3. Is deployed where someone can actually use it

## The three-project rule

You don't need ten projects. You need **three excellent ones** that each demonstrate a different muscle:

### Project 1 — An end-to-end ML application

Pick a real problem (predict something useful, classify something messy) and take it all the way: data cleaning, model training, evaluation, and a deployed interface. The deployment is non-negotiable — a model nobody can click is a model nobody believes.

### Project 2 — Something with modern LLMs

Build a retrieval-augmented app or a small agent that uses tools. This shows you're current. Bonus points if it solves a problem you personally have.

### Project 3 — An evaluation or analysis project

This is the rare one that makes you stand out. Take a model (yours or an API) and *evaluate* it rigorously: define a rubric, test it on real cases, and write up where it fails. Evaluation skills are in huge demand — so much so that there's an entire market of <Link href="/ai-training-jobs">AI evaluation jobs</Link>.

<Callout type="success">
**The write-up is half the project.** For each one, publish a short README that explains the problem, your approach, what didn't work, and what you'd do next. This is what interviewers actually read.
</Callout>

## Make your work credible

- **Deploy it.** Use any free hosting. A live link beats a screenshot every time.
- **Show the evaluation.** "87% accuracy on a held-out test set" says more than "it works well."
- **Write honestly.** Admitting a limitation signals maturity, not weakness.
- **Use version control.** A clean commit history tells its own story about how you work.

## Turn real experience into portfolio gold

Here's a shortcut most people miss: **paid AI training and evaluation work is portfolio experience.** When you spend weeks rating model outputs or labeling data, you learn exactly how models fail — insight you can turn into a sharp evaluation project. You also get a real line on your résumé.

Pair your three projects with structured skill-building — our <Link href="/roadmaps/ai-engineer">AI Engineer roadmap</Link> and <Link href="/technologies/ai">AI technology guides</Link> will fill the gaps — and you'll have something most applicants don't: proof.

In AI hiring, proof wins. Build three things you're proud of, deploy them, write them up honestly, and let your work make the argument for you.
